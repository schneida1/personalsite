Case study 01 · United Nations Global Compact

From days of reading to minutes of review.

The Global Compact collects sustainability reports from thousands of member companies. Turning them into something decision-makers can use was slow, manual, and depended on a few people's stamina.

Role
Product Owner, Reporting
Since
July 2024
Users
Analysts & member orgs worldwide
Stack
LLMs · React

The problem

Each reporting cycle, analysts read hundreds of long, inconsistently structured reports and synthesized them by hand. It took days, quality depended on who did it, and errors in submitted data were usually caught late, by someone happening to notice.

Key decisions

  • Automate the synthesis, keep a human on the conclusion. The model drafts summaries and structured extracts; an analyst reviews and signs off. People's time moves from reading to judging. Trade-offSlower than fully automatic, but accuracy holds up and analysts trust the output enough to use it.
  • Catch bad data at the door, not at the end. Rebuilt the validation layer with AI-assisted rules, so problems are flagged when a report is submitted instead of weeks later.
  • Write specs people can read, then prototype from them. Plain-language requirements that also generate working React components, so stakeholders react to something real, not a document.
  • Let usage drive the roadmap. Set up engagement, task-completion, and adoption metrics from scratch, and ran a global early-adopter program whose feedback actually changed what shipped.

Outcome

  • Report synthesis that took days now takes minutes, at the same accuracy.
  • Data problems surface automatically at submission.
  • A roadmap tied to real usage instead of a quarterly planning doc.
Case study 02 · Betawise

288 readings a day. One thing to work on.

I have Type 1 diabetes. My CGM produces a reading every five minutes, and I see an endocrinologist for about fifteen minutes a few times a year. Betawise is the layer in between that I wanted for myself.

Role
Founder · product & build
For
People with T1D & T2D
Data
Dexcom · Clarity · LibreView
Live
betawise.org ↗

The problem

CGMs made glucose data free; understanding it is still a privilege. About 4,000 readings pile up every two weeks in apps that show a squiggly line and an average. The clinical reports are written for doctors. The person living with it gets an overload of data and no plan.

Key decisions

  • One weekly focus, not a dashboard. The pattern engine finds everything (overnight lows, dawn phenomenon, post-meal spikes, rebound highs) but shows one thing to work on. Less to think about means more follow-through. Trade-offHides detail power users want, so the full metrics are one tap away.
  • Safety always ranks first. If lows exceed consensus limits, that's the focus, even when highs would gain more time in range. Try it in the demo →
  • AI explains the numbers; it never invents them. Every metric uses a published, peer-reviewed definition: the International Consensus on Time in Range, the Glycemia Risk Index, and GMI. The AI coach talks about them; the math is deterministic and tested.
  • Your readings never leave your device. Analysis runs in the browser. The coach gets a de-identified summary: no raw readings, dates, or device IDs. No account required.
  • Guardrails that don't depend on the model. Dosing questions, emergencies, and crisis language are caught by fixed rules before any AI call, and get fixed, safe responses.

What shipped

  • Dexcom connection plus Clarity / LibreView CSV import
  • Consensus metrics, Glycemia Risk Index, and an Ambulatory Glucose Profile
  • Pattern engine and a ranked weekly focus
  • AI peer coach with structured weekly insights
  • A one-page, clinic-ready report to bring to appointments
Case study 03 · Jarvis

The best input is the one you already use.

Every notes app asks you to open it, choose a folder, and fill in a form. But the fastest thing anyone does on a phone is send a text. Jarvis is a notes app you use by texting yourself.

Role
Designer & builder
Platform
iPhone · web sync
Stack
SwiftUI · SwiftData · App Intents
Status
In daily personal use

The problem

Thoughts show up at bad times: walking, in a meeting, half-asleep. Every tap between the thought and saving it loses some of them, and anything that does get saved still has to be organized later.

Key decisions

  • Syntax instead of forms. #tags, "remind me…", "- sub-items", and #event are parsed from plain text. There are no fields to fill in; the message itself sets the structure. Trade-offHard to discover, so the app gives examples and the syntax is forgiving ("at 9am", "tomorrow 3pm", "in 30 minutes").
  • Capture from anywhere. Siri, the Action button, Shortcuts, a Messages automation (text yourself in iMessage and it's filed), and a dedicated phone number via Twilio and a Cloudflare Worker.
  • One store, many views. Feed, Board (tags become kanban columns), To-do, and Calendar all read from one data store, so they always agree.
  • Offline-first, sync second. The phone keeps a full copy and syncs with the web version in the background; if a note is edited in two places, the latest edit wins. Photos stay on the device.
  • Glanceable, not openable. Lock-screen widgets and a Live Activity show today's reminders, so most of the time you never need to open the app.

What shipped

  • Natural-language reminders as local notifications
  • #event notes added straight to Apple Calendar
  • Permanent IDs (N-0024) so notes can be referenced
  • Lock screen, Dynamic Island, Siri, and Shortcuts support
  • Web sync and clean CSV export
How I work

Four rules I keep coming back to.

01

Meet people at the habit they already have.

New behavior is expensive. I'd rather fit into a habit people already have than ask them to learn a new one.

In practice: Jarvis works over text messages; Betawise reads the CGM export people already have.

02

Show less, so people act more.

A dashboard with twelve numbers gets admired and then ignored. One clear next step gets done.

In practice: Betawise's single weekly focus, with the full detail one tap away.

03

AI for the reading, people for the judgment.

Models are great at turning lots of text into something useful, but they shouldn't make the final call where it matters. I design that boundary on purpose.

In practice: UN analysts sign off on AI synthesis; Betawise's safety rules run before any model call.

04

Prototype before you persuade.

A working thing settles in ten minutes what a document argues about for ten days. I build what I'm proposing.

In practice: Specs that generate React components at the UN; both side projects built end to end.
